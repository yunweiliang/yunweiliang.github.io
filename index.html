This exercise examines free will in the context of John Steinbeck's East of Eden. Specifically, Claude and I are inspecting Steinbeck's own biblical Cain: Caleb Trask.

Day 0 Research

I knew I wanted to dissect literary texts with Claude. Something around linguistics, classic literature, or language. And I ran into a lot of problems on the first day just to get started.

AI is often used for creation. Only in the past year did I learn more about its application in answering research problems with the scientific method.

Researchers have used Claude for everything from running calculations on famous proofs to reconstructing a map of the sky under different lights. Learning more about the scientific method, I realized working with AI is like working with a really, really smart PhD student. This would be a good opportunity for me to run my own research on any problems that are actually interesting to me.

I wanted to investigate classic literature. I love reading.

Deciding on an actionable problem to work on was the hardest part. Most problems are too trivial, with deterministic solutions. I wanted to work on a problem that is partly non-deterministic.

The other issue I ran into is around eval. I am most familiar with classic literature, but the model was likely trained on them. Any fact-checking agent would have to use a golden set using publications after the model is trained, which makes verification a bit more difficult. I would have to read 50+ short essays and books published after the model was trained (e.g., June 2026 for Opus 5.5). Nor did I want to delegate grader to another agent without validating the dev-set myself. Actual eval would take months.

Most of today was spent talking to the agent about what datasets are available. Asking for its opinion.

I ended up restricting the problem space to one book only. Instead of asking about what happened, how it happened, I asked what could have happened instead.

Warning: spoiler ahead.

Prompt

I landed on the subject of Timshel: that people can choose how to be despite how they are supposed to be. What would be the permutation of events such that Caleb never retaliates against Aron?

I want this to be more than an intuitive interpretation: "if Adam took Caleb's money, then Caleb would not tell Aron."

While I agree with this fact, I want this plotted. I want the agent to convince me. I want the model to draw out the alternative plot line.

  1. Model proposes 30 significant events

First pass, the proposer was mostly accurate drawing from memory the events significant to the plot. I corrected minor hallucinated events, e.g. when it confused East of Eden's 1955 movie's edited plot vs. the actual book's events. I largely agreed with the judge's final filtered list.

The judge agreed 88% of the time across 5 runs. Acceptable, but I realized the premise is wrong. While this graph explains how events transpired, it doesn't explain why Caleb acted the way he did.

I revised both proposer and judge to filter for events significant to the character instead. Focus on events that built his perception of self, motivations, perpetual fear, and introspection.

Based on the second collection I selected a few in my own notes for what the judge should be keeping then passed it to the judge to filter. Most events I selected were also included by the judge. These were events that mostly happened to Caleb Trask indirectly or directly.

  • Cal discovers that the madam Kate is his mother (ch. 38, judge kept 5/5): Cal learns his mother is a brothel keeper and fears her evil is in him.
  • Adam rejects Cal's money and praises Aron instead (ch. 49, judge kept 5/5): Cal feels utterly rejected by his father and blinded by jealousy of Aron.

The judge kept 8 more events that I cut, for example:

  • Cal prays not to be mean (ch. 27, judge kept 4/5): the prayer shows Cal already fearing he is mean by nature and aching to be loved like Aron.
  • Lee tells Cal the truth and that his choices are his own (ch. 39, judge kept 5/5): Cal acts without the excuse of bad blood and carries full responsibility.

Judge disagreed with me on events based on what Caleb came to believe about himself. This is a gap I missed and I think the judge is correct. I hypothesize a person's inner state can self-manifest, and in his case, fester the more he confirmed ingrained beliefs he had about himself.

Comparing the two graphs, the former could have made a best seller, but the latter built the moral spine of East of Eden.
